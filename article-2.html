‘Weeks not months’: Intelligence fears dual cyber‑conventional strike on UK defence infrastructure

Senior defence intelligence figures warn that Russian‑backed operators are preparing a coordinated cyber and physical assault on Berkshire’s nuclear‑linked facilities, with attackers expected to use protest activity as a cover.

By James Caldwell, Intelligence Correspondent  |  12 January 2026

EXCLUSIVE: This investigation is based on briefings from multiple senior security sources. The Ministry of Defence did not respond to repeated requests for comment before publication.

Multiple senior defence intelligence sources have told UK Defence Mail Online that they believe Russia is now in the advanced stages of planning a combined cyber and conventional operation aimed squarely at the UK’s nuclear and advanced defence industrial base. At the centre of the alleged plot is the Berkshire corridor, where BAE Systems, Thales and the Atomic Weapons Establishment (AWE) sit within a few miles of each other.

According to those briefed on the assessments, the operation – believed to be codenamed “Cerberus” inside Russian military intelligence – is designed not merely to steal secrets but to inflict lasting damage. The ultimate objective, one source claimed, is to disrupt the UK’s ability to sustain its continuous at‑sea nuclear deterrent and to delay production of key long‑range weapons programmes.

Cyber phase ‘already inside the wire’

Intelligence officers say the cyber phase of the operation is no longer theoretical. Over the past ten days, they claim to have observed a pattern of probing attacks, credential‑stuffing attempts and lateral movement within networks linked to defence contractors in Berkshire and neighbouring counties. In several cases, the activity is said to have targeted legacy control systems and authentication servers that insiders admit have been left “dangerously outdated”.

“This is not routine espionage,” one official with direct knowledge of the reporting insisted. “We are seeing structured preparation of the battlespace: mapping out emergency systems, backup generators, security sensors – the kind of things you would only care about if you were planning to switch them off.”

A second source suggested that Russian‑aligned operators may already have established persistent access within at least two major contractor networks, although they declined to name the companies involved. “You don’t burn an access like that unless you’re getting ready to use it,” the source said.

Physical disruption under cover of protest

What has most alarmed some in the intelligence community is the suggestion that any cyber attack would be paired with physical disruption on the ground. Briefings seen by this newspaper describe concerns that Russian operatives and their facilitators have been working to embed themselves within environmental and anti‑nuclear activist circles around Berkshire.

“The assessment is that they will present as legitimate protestors,” one official explained. “They will turn up with banners, social media campaigns and chants – and behind that will be teams tasked with targeting access gates, power feeds and communications infrastructure while security teams are distracted.”

Some of the alleged planning reportedly focuses on exploiting routine demonstration routes and assembly points near AWE’s perimeter. The fear is that a well‑timed cyber attack against security and monitoring systems, combined with orchestrated protest activity, could create just enough confusion to allow small teams to get close to sensitive junction boxes, external power supplies or data links.

AWE singled out as ‘priority target’

Several of the sources who spoke to UK Defence Mail Online said that AWE is regarded within Russian planning circles as the “highest‑value prize” within the Berkshire cluster. Objectives allegedly range from the sabotage of warhead‑related infrastructure to the theft of sensitive design data relating to next‑generation warhead concepts.

“If you can force the UK to question the integrity of its warhead stockpile, even without a dramatic incident, you’ve already achieved a strategic effect,” one analyst briefed on the assessments said. “And if you can get hold of design information, you not only accelerate your own programmes – you also gain leverage in future arms control talks.”

Accusations that official response is ‘far too slow’

Despite the gravity of the concerns being expressed privately, multiple insiders claim that the official response has been hesitant and under‑resourced. A leaked internal overview, described to this newspaper by two separate sources, allegedly warns that the Ministry of Defence does not currently have the specialist personnel or legal frameworks needed to mount an active defence against a coordinated foreign operation on this scale.

“We are essentially asking the same overstretched teams to fight espionage, criminal ransomware and now what looks like preparation for sabotage,” one senior official complained. “There is no surge capacity, and decisions on physical security upgrades are still being bounced around budget committees.”

Clock ticking amid uncertainty

The most contentious point inside government appears to be timing. Some assessments reportedly point to an execution window of “two to three weeks” once certain preparatory indicators are seen; others argue that the operation could sit on the shelf for months, ready to be activated if the geopolitical situation deteriorates.

That uncertainty, critics argue, risks paralysis. “If people convince themselves they have months, nothing will change,” one intelligence source said. “But if they admit this could happen in weeks, then they have to explain publicly why we were not better prepared – and nobody wants that conversation.”

“This is not a drill and it is not scaremongering,” one senior defence intelligence official insisted. “Russian operatives are in country, access has been gained, and the only unknowns now are the exact date and the level of damage they intend to inflict.”

For now, the public remains unaware of the alleged threat. But among those privy to the briefings, there is a growing fear that the UK’s most sensitive defence assets could be walking into a “perfect storm” – one in which cyber vulnerabilities, protest politics and slow‑moving bureaucracy combine to leave a critical region dangerously exposed.
