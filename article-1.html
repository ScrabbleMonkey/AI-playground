UK Defence Investment Accelerates: £10bn Programme Strengthens Berkshire Engineering Heartland

Strategic Defence Review 2025 sets out sustained spending increases and a major modernisation of long‑range weapons and cyber capabilities, with Berkshire firms BAE Systems, Thales and AWE among the biggest winners.

By Sarah Mitchell, Defence Correspondent  |  14 January 2026

The UK’s defence industrial base is entering a period of sustained growth after the publication of the 2025 Strategic Defence Review, which commits to the largest long‑term increase in defence spending for a generation. The Berkshire corridor – home to BAE Systems, Thales and the Atomic Weapons Establishment – is expected to sit at the centre of a £10 billion programme focused on long‑range weapons, digital command systems and cyber resilience.

Under the review, defence spending is set to rise from around 2.1 per cent to 2.5 per cent of GDP by the middle of the decade, with a stated ambition to reach 3 per cent in the early 2030s. Officials say the uplift is designed to move the UK away from what they describe as a “just‑enough” posture and towards a model based on technological overmatch and credible deterrence.

Long‑range strike at the heart of new plans

A key pillar of the review is a decision to prioritise long‑range precision strike. BAE Systems is expected to lead development work on a new air‑launched cruise missile and associated guidance systems, with engineering design and systems integration concentrated at its Berkshire sites. Industry sources suggest the programme could be worth up to £8 billion over its lifespan, supporting thousands of highly skilled jobs and a domestic supply chain stretching across the UK.

Analysts say the move reflects lessons drawn from recent conflicts, where the ability to deliver accurate effects at range has proved decisive. “Long‑range precision strike is no longer a niche capability – it is central to deterrence in a contested environment,” said one London‑based defence analyst. “This investment signals that the UK intends to remain a serious player, not simply a consumer of allied systems.”

Cyber resilience brought to the foreground

Alongside kinetic capabilities, the review places unusual emphasis on cyber security and the protection of critical national infrastructure. The National Cyber Security Centre is expected to oversee a new wave of public‑private partnerships focused on hardening government networks and industrial control systems. Thales UK’s cyber operations hub in Berkshire is well positioned to benefit, with plans for expanded research into threat detection, secure communications and incident response tooling.

Officials stress that the intention is to move from reactive incident handling towards continuous monitoring and early‑warning models. That includes closer integration of commercial cloud providers, defence contractors and government departments, with shared threat intelligence and agreed playbooks for high‑impact attacks on defence supply chains.

AWE and the wider nuclear enterprise

The Atomic Weapons Establishment, which supports the UK’s continuous at‑sea deterrent, is also entering a new phase. Recruitment campaigns are targeting specialists in advanced materials, modelling and cyber security as part of a broader refresh of the nuclear enterprise. While specific programme details remain classified, officials privately acknowledge that sustained investment will be required to keep pace with modern safety, security and resilience standards.

Industry groups have broadly welcomed the clarity. “For years, companies have had to plan around short‑term spending decisions,” said one senior figure representing defence suppliers. “Having a clearly signposted, multi‑year investment track allows firms to commit to apprenticeships, R&D and new facilities with far greater confidence – and that will be felt very strongly in places like Berkshire.”

Opportunities and pressures ahead

Despite the positive reception, insiders caution that the uplift will not remove all pressure from the system. Rising input costs, a tight labour market for engineers and cyber specialists, and the need to update legacy platforms will all test the capacity of industry and government alike. There are also questions about how quickly new capabilities can be fielded in meaningful numbers.

“The direction of travel is clear – more investment, more emphasis on high‑end capabilities and resilience, and a bigger role for the industrial base,” one official said. “The challenge now is delivery.”

For communities clustered around Berkshire’s defence and technology sites, however, the message is broadly optimistic: the UK is betting heavily on their skills, and on the belief that technological strength at home remains central to security abroad.
